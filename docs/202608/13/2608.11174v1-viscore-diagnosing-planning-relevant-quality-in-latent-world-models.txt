# ABSTRACT 

Regulating the latent space to an isotropic Gaussian distribution provides a stable and information-maximized landscape for world model planning. However, the latent space property and successful planning remain disconnected. We first study this by comparing SIGReg and VISReg, two regularization loss functions with the same distribution target but different properties. Compared with SIGReg, VISReg has more flexibility in controlling the weights of center, scale, and shape regu-larization, and a larger batch size brings a finer distribution approximation. We find that the former, despite being beneficial in self-supervised learning (SSL), does not help the planning, whereas the latter improves the planning success on out-of-domain (OOD) datasets. This motivates a deep understanding of the fac-tors that correlate with the success rate. Unlike the previous metrics focusing on the encoded latent only, we propose the Veracity-Influence-Sobriety score (VIS-core), a metric that quantifies the reachability and capacity of a predictor given the encoded feature, and the hallucination of the searching-based planner. Com-pared with straightness, physical-state probing, and empowerment, we show that, with the measurement covering encoder, predictor, and planner, VIScore explains the success rate better than the others, as reflected by a strong Spearman corre-lation. Specifically, VIScore consistently achieves a Spearman correlation over 0.75 on both seen and unseen models and datasets on the cross-task success rate pool. Moreover, VIScore is the only metric that has a calibration error below the constant fit across all testing scenarios, showcasing the importance of these three aspects in planning success. We hope this metric can help future studies on world model design and diagnosis. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

The data efficiency and lightweight training characteristics make Joint-Embedding Predictive Ar-chitecture (JEPA) an attractive approach for world-model training (Maes et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Assran et al., 2025). A recent example, LeWorldModel (LeWM) creates a simple yet effective training paradigm — consisting of an image encoder, an action encoder, and a predictor — to achieve a competitive performance on various datasets. Although LeWM has a few latent quality analyses, they are disconnected from the predictor and planner, which are essential to planning success. This paper is meant to bridge the latent quality and success rate. We study the first question through a controlled regularization intervention. LeWM relies on SI-GReg (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025) to prevent collapse during training. We replace SIGReg with VISReg (Wu et al., 2026), which targets the same isotropic Gaussian distribution but separates cen-ter, scale, and shape constraints. In SSL, this separation yields a stronger corrective gradient near collapse and allows the components to be reweighted on long-tailed or low-rank data. With the same architecture and planning protocol, VIS-WorldModel (VIS-WM) achieves a competitive success rate on PushT (Zhou et al., 2025), Reacher (Tassa et al., 2018), OGBench-Cube (Park et al., 2025), and Two-Room (Sobal et al., 2025); yet, its reweighting flexibility does not yield a stronger planning result, whereas a more accurate distribution matching improves OOD planning. This indicates the 1arXiv:2608.11174v1 [cs.RO] 11 Aug 2026 theory of the ideal latent distribution for unseen tasks proposed in LeJEPA (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025) transfers to world model planning, but it does not explain why the planning succeeds or fails. Continuing the analysis, we focus on the exist-ing metrics for measur-ing the latent feature of world models: Straight-ness characterizes trajec-tory geometry (Wang et al., 2026); physical-state prob-ing evaluates the neces-sary physical information in the current latent (Maes et al., 2026); and empow-erment measures action-conditioned capacity (Klyubin et al., 2005). None of them analyzes the predictor, planner, and success tolerance, which are the components executing the planning process and condition deciding the planning success. In light of the above, we propose the Veracity–Influence–Sobriety score (VIScore). Veracity mea-sures whether open-loop rollout error remains within the task’s own success tolerance. Influence 

reformulates latent empowerment as a sufficiency condition that saturates once action-conditioned capacity exceeds the level needed by the task. Sobriety measures whether the planner overly discov-ers improvements over an expert plan ( e.g. , action labels), thereby probing planner exploitation of predictor error. These three factors diagnose complementary failure modes: unreachable imagined dynamics, insufficient action-conditioned capacity, and exploitable hallucination. Their product re-quires all three conditions to hold simultaneously. To evaluate the efficacy, we use success rate as an external validation criterion. We report the Spear-man correlation and calibration error (Zadrozny & Elkan, 2002) between success rate and existing metric scores (including VIScore) on the held-out checkpoint pool, held-out training method pool, and held-out dataset pool. VIScore consistently has the best Spearman correlation ( i.e. , ≥ 0.75 

) in cross-task evaluation and is the only metric that achieves a lower calibration error than the simple constant predictor. Moreover, we extend the same experiment to other sampling-based plan-ners (Rubinstein, 1999; Pinneri et al., 2020; Williams et al., 2017; Howell et al., 2022) and VIScore consistently achieves the most stable and competitive results, indicating a good application range in world-model training diagnosis and evaluation. The contributions of this paper are summarized as: • We use a controlled SIGReg–VISReg study to show that self-supervised advantages do not au-tomatically transfer to latent planning: component flexibility brings no planning gain, while the realized accuracy of distribution matching affects OOD performance. • We introduce three interpretable diagnostic axes of planning-relevant latent world-model quality—veracity, influence, and sobriety—and combine them into the VIScore. This is the first metric measuring the quality of components that directly relate to planning success: encoder, predictor, and planner. • We validate the diagnostics against planning success and show the efficacy of VIScore in differ-entiating models with different levels of success rates. This provides strong evidence of VIScore being useful as a diagnostic tool in world model method development. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

Training stability in latent world models. Latent world models stabilize representation and dy-namics learning in different ways. DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2025) predicts over frozen pretrained features, whereas PLDM (Sobal et al., 2025), GCBC (Ghosh et al., 2019), and GCIQL (Kostrikov et al., 2021) couple representation learning to goal-reaching, behavioral cloning, or value objectives. LeWorldModel (Maes et al., 2026) instead trains its encoder and predictor jointly from pixels and uses SIGReg (Balestriero & LeCun, 2025) to avoid representational collapse. VISReg (Wu et al., 2026) targets the same isotropic Gaussian prior while separating center, scale, and shape constraints, 2making the SIGReg–VISReg substitution a controlled test of which self-supervised properties re-main consequential once the representation is used for planning. 

Diagnostics for latent planning. World models are commonly evaluated with prediction losses or representation-level summaries, although lower prediction error need not yield better control (Lam-bert et al., 2020). Physical-state probes and spectral statistics measure encoded information (Maes et al., 2026; Garrido et al., 2023; Agrawal et al., 2022; Thilak et al., 2024); straightness measures latent trajectory geometry (Wang et al., 2026); and empowerment measures action-conditioned ca-pacity (Klyubin et al., 2005; Mohamed & Rezende, 2015; Zhao et al., 2021; Song et al., 2025). These quantities do not jointly test the predictor and the planner that consume the representation. VIScore complements them with three planning-specific diagnostics—task-scaled rollout fidelity, sufficient action influence, and resistance to search exploitation—and is validated by how strongly and consistently it is associated with planning success.

# 4 VIS CORE : D IAGNOSING PLANNING -R ELEVANT MODEL QUALITY 

Predictor-based planning is achieved by a vision encoder, action encoder, predictor, and planner, but the existing diagnostics only focus on one or two components: straightness measures the geometry produced by the vision encoder; physical-state probing measures the physical information contained in the latent produced by the vision encoder; empowerment is calculated based on the output of both the vision encoder and predictor. This makes these metrics off from becoming a stable diagnostic when model fails to plan. This section describes the proposed metric, VIScore, which measures the quality of vision encoder, predictor, and planner, so that insights can be provided for diagnosis. 4.1 DEFINITION 

Predictor-based planning requires three conditions: i) the predictor should roll the latent state for-ward accurately enough for the task; ii) different actions should produce sufficiently distinguishable futures; iii) the planner should not produce a large fraction of candidates that have a lower cost than action labels. We refer to these conditions as veracity , influence , and sobriety , respectively. Veracity is the probability that an H-step open-loop rollout error stays inside the ball where the task credits as success. The same absolute latent error is harmless for coarse navigation and fatal for manipulation, so the factor is a ratio , dtol /σ roll , and only that ratio enters: rescaling the latent mul-tiplies numerator and denominator alike, which is what makes veracity comparable across models of different latent dimension and scale. The error function converts the ratio into [0 , 1) by reading it as the mass an isotropic Gaussian error of scale σroll places inside the tolerance ball in one dimen-sion. We do not claim the rollout error is Gaussian; erf is used as a bounded monotone map, and because every within-task correlation we report is a rank statistic, three alternative monotone maps give rank-identical results. More details are in Appendix B.1. 

Influence counts, in nats, how many H-step futures the actions can steer to that remain distinguish-able above the predictor’s own noise floor. It is latent empowerment (Klyubin et al., 2005)—the channel capacity from action perturbations to the future state, used elsewhere as an intrinsic control objective (Mohamed & Rezende, 2015; Zhao et al., 2021; Song et al., 2025)—with the local linear-Gaussian estimator of Appendix B.1. VIScore is the product of these three components, meaning that a good model should satisfy all of them together. We want to clarify a potential misunderstanding of what VIScore is. It is a score measuring the reachability, capacity, and exploitable hallucination of the model. Despite the high correlation with success rate shown in the following subsection, a high or low VIScore does not always guarantee a successful or failed planning because there are so many other factors ( e.g. ,environment factors, complexity of the task) can affect this. Hence, VIScore should be treated as a diagnostic rather than a regression of model performance. 4.2 EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS

VIScore is strongly associated with planning success. Table 6 evaluates checkpoints from the original training family. Within each task, VIScore and raw empowerment are comparable on the development pool ( +0 .80 / + 0 .83 / + 0 .71 against +0 .82 / + 0 .86 / + 0 .71 on PushT/Reacher/Two-Room), and both exceed the physical-state probe and straightness, which changes sign across tasks. The separation appears across tasks. Empowerment’s pooled correlation collapses to +0 .40 while VIScore reaches +0 .88 , and its calibration error is 23 .1 points against VIScore’s 10 .2. This suggests that a given number of nats does not mean the same planning quality on two tasks, whereas a capacity converted into a sufficiency condition and multiplied by veracity and sobriety does. VIScore is also the only metric whose calibration interval lies entirely below the constant-predictor. 8The held-out pool is the central test, because its 33 training runs share nothing with the 14 runs on which every constant was chosen. Every checkpoint here carries the same three evaluation seeds, so the arms are paired with the planner comparison of Appendix B.6. The relationship transfers intact: VIScore reaches +0 .84 / + 0 .72 / + 0 .83 within task, +0 .91 pooled, and 7.0 points of calibration error against the reference 18 .2. Both cross-task measures are in fact better here than on the devel-opment pool where the constants were fitted, which is the opposite of what leakage would produce. Empowerment is a genuinely strong within-task competitor— +0 .82 on Reacher and +0 .86 on Two-Room, and statistically indistinguishable from VIScore on five of the six within-task cells of the two pools—which indicates that the baseline is not artificially weak; what it cannot do is carry one scale across tasks, where it falls to +0 .49 pooled and 15 .6 points, above the constant reference.

checkpoints from four held-out methods, each labelled under nine evaluation seeds with our own planner and protocol, VIScore attains the strongest association of the four diagnostics ( +0 .75 ) and is the only one that beats the frozen development calibration map’s reference ( 8.3 against 11 .3). No baseline manages both: raw empowerment is nearly unrelated to success here ( +0 .12 ) yet calibrates to 12 .0 points, above the reference, and the physical-state probe reaches only +0 .32 at 16 .1 points. On 20 checkpoints from the unseen MAZE task, VIScore again ranks best, and its frozen calibration error of 11 .5 points is far below the constant reference ( 41 .7)—though here raw empowerment is better calibrated still, at 9.2, because MAZE operates well below the influence cap ( 20 .3 against 

82 nats) so an uncapped capacity retains informative scale. The application range therefore covers method and task shifts for ordering , while one universal numerical map from score to success is supported for the method shift and a new task family. Further discussions are in Appendix B.4. 

The three factors are complementary. No single factor is best on every pool: the strongest one changes with the checkpoint regime, and each fails somewhere the other two do not. Because the identity of the binding factor is what a diagnosis has to report, the factors are meant to be read individually; Appendix B.5 scores each alone on all five pools. 

VIScore fits into any searching-based planner. The above results are done with CEM planner, which is limited. We tested four more popular searching-based planners and the results support that VIScore works well for searching-based planners. Details are in Appendix B.6. 

Scope. Although VIScore has a stronger correlation with success rate than the other methods, it is a diagnostic rather than the predictor of success rate. A model achieving a high VIScore does not mean the model can successfully plan to the target. For example, the models trained on OGBench-Cube can achieve a good VIScore but it has an opposite correlation to success rate. The reason is that the success of gripping in OGBench-Cube directly decide whether the planning can be successful, and such a discrete outcome is not measured in VIScore. In addition, VIScore does not fit into amortized planners because their planning do not include a predictor at all. Reachability, capacity, and exploitable hallucination of the predictor-based world model are what measured by VIScore. 

# 5 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

This paper tries to bridge latent space properties and the success rate of the predictor-based world model. First, we analyze the effect of the regularization loss used in the world model training. This reveals that a more accurate distribution regularization brings a better OOD performance. Second, we propose VIScore, a metric that measures the reachability, capacity, and exploitable hallucination of world model planning. We used success rate as the external signal to evaluate this metric from various aspects. The results support that VIScore covers important factors for planning success. During the analysis, we find a few leftover problems that should be noticed to the community: 1) single-seed evaluation brings a huge bias on success rate, so multi-seed evaluation is needed; 2) OOD and long-horizon evaluations should be mandatory because the current benchmark is originated from the same environment as training; 3) the diagnostic toolkit for IDM/amortized planning remains empty; 4) multi-task planning remains untouched; 5) existing methods saturate on the commonly used datasets in short-horizon, so more complex training sets are needed; 6) training without using action labels is still difficult. 9REFERENCES

The split is at run level, and what that costs. We score every training epoch of the runs of Section 3, excluding the cls -reconstruction ablation arm. Each training run is then assigned entirely to the development fold or to the test side; no run contributes checkpoints to both. Development is where every constant is chosen— τ , the dtol recipe, the calibration maps—so this guarantees that no constant was tuned on a sibling epoch of a test checkpoint, which a split at epoch or evaluation-seed level would not. The assignment is not arbitrary, because a naive one destroys the development pool. Moving every run that carries fresh-seed labels to the test side leaves development with 117 checkpoints from 12 

runs and drops its own label resolvability to 2.2/1.6/1.9, i.e. below the threshold we require of any pool we correlate against. We therefore take a stratified half: runs are ordered by their fresh-seed contribution and moved to the test side, round-robin over (task, objective) so that neither side is single-task or single-objective, and the transfer stops before development would fall under a spread-over-standard-error of 2.2 on any task or below 20 checkpoints on any task. The resulting folds are 

14 development runs against 38 test runs, with an objective mix of 11 VISReg and 3 SIGReg on the development side and 27 against 6 on the test side. The cost is borne almost entirely by development, which falls from 472 checkpoints to 137 while keeping resolvability 2.2/3.4/2.4 on the three tasks; the test pools lose little ( 4 cells from 2 runs are discarded from the held-out fold because their runs stayed in development). Development’s own calibration error rises from 8.5 to 10 .2 points under the split, which we report as the honest number: it is the price of the guarantee, not a degradation of the metric. The test pool moves the other way— held-out calibration improves to 7.0 points and its pooled correlation to +0 .91 —so the run-level guarantee is obtained without weakening any conclusion. 

Bootstrap unit. Epoch checkpoints of one training run are not independent samples: they share an initialization, a data order and a success level, and development draws 137 checkpoints from only 14 

runs. Resampling checkpoints would therefore understate every interval. All intervals in this paper are cluster bootstraps over training runs ( 2000 resamples): a run is drawn with replacement and all of its checkpoints enter the resample together. For VIScore this gives pooled +0 .88 [+0 .78 , +0 .93] 

and calibration 10 .2 [7 .4, 16 .3] on development, and +0 .91 [+0 .84 , +0 .93] and 7.0 [5 .3, 10 .8] on held-out. The corresponding intervals for raw empowerment are [18 .7, 28 .2] and [12 .2, 19 .5] for calibration and +0 .49 [+0 .33 , +0 .70] for held-out pooled, so VIScore’s calibration interval lies en-tirely below the constant reference on both pools while empowerment’s does on neither. 

Cube needs a factor VIScore does not measure. Cube is the one task whose success is decided by something none of the three factors represents. An episode succeeds only if the gripper enters a closed mode on the block, a discrete transition, whereas veracity, influence and sobriety are all continuous measurements of a continuous latent. The factor values say as much: Cube’s median 

memp is 236 nats against a cap of 82 , so influence is numerically equal to 1 on every Cube checkpoint in both pools and one third of the product carries no information at all. A diagnostic for this task would need a mode-conditioned factor—whether the predictor represents the transition, and whether search can fabricate it—which we do not have. We therefore report Cube’s within-task column in parentheses and exclude it from Pooled , Calib. and Ref. , and we read its entries as a missing axis rather than as a failure of the axes we do measure. 

Estimating dtol . Among probe pairs one to five frames apart, we retain those whose task-scored physical displacement lies within 20% of the success tolerance. This is the trade-off the cap was chosen for, paid on a task family where it happens not to bind. B.5 FACTOR ABLATION 

The product in Eq. 4 encodes the hypothesis that all three conditions matter. Table 9 tests whether one factor alone is sufficient. We use the same association and calibration protocol as in the corre-sponding full-score experiment and do not select a different factor for each pool. The conclusion is that no individual factor dominates every shift. Sobriety is strongest where en-coded information and rollout capacity vary little but a localized predictor error can still be amplified by search—it leads on development ( +0 .89 , 9.3 points) and MAZE ( +0 .88 , 8.3), and is the best-calibrated factor on the held-out checkpoints ( 7.3). Influence becomes competitive where the pool spans collapsed, undertrained and healthy representations, because capacity has not yet saturated (+0 .87 on MAZE, whose median capacity is 20 .3 nats against the 82 -nat cap), and it goes silent where capacity has saturated. Veracity binds when the task’s precision requirement is tight. Because the identity of the binding factor is exactly what a diagnosis has to report, the factors are meant to be read individually; the product is the summary that never falls below +0 .75 on any pool, and on the held-out checkpoints it is the best column on both cross-task measures ( +0 .91 , 7.0 points). It is fixed in advance rather than selected after observing each pool: the fixed product is less locally optimal than post-hoc factor selection, but its components retain a consistent diagnostic interpretation. B.6 TRANSFER ACROSS PLANNER FAMILIES 

Sobriety explicitly contains a search procedure, raising the question of whether VIScore is specific to CEM. We keep the checkpoint, goals, episode count, and planning cost fixed and replace only the optimizer. The six planners are CEM, MPPI (Williams et al., 2017), iCEM (Pinneri et al., 2020), predictive sampling (Howell et al., 2022), multi-start AdamW through the predictor, and single-start AdamW. They span sampling- and gradient-based search with budgets from 100 to 9000 model 17 evaluations. Because every arm must be evaluated on the same checkpoints for the comparison to be paired, this experiment uses a fixed 36 -checkpoint subset drawn from the training runs of the pools in Section 4: all 25 of its runs appear there, 20 of the 36 checkpoints are themselves cells of the development or held-out pool, and the remaining 16 are other epochs of those same runs, spanning a wider quality range than any single pool does. The set is therefore a subset of the main paper’s runs rather than of its reported checkpoints. Veracity and influence remain unchanged across planners. Sobriety is recomputed using an as-signment fixed before observing the results: sampling planners use a mini-CEM probe, multi-start gradient descent uses a mini multi-start AdamW probe, and single-start gradient descent uses a single-start probe. Each probe uses a small fraction of the corresponding deployed budget. No planner-specific probe is selected according to correlation. VIScore has positive within-task association in all 18 planner–task cells, from +0 .29 to +0 .91 with a median of +0 .67 , showing that the checkpoint relationship is not an artifact of CEM. It also does not decay as the planner weakens: the rank correlation between a planner’s per-task success deficit against CEM and its within-task ρ is +0 .07 over those 18 cells, and predictive sampling reaches 

43 .3 on PushT against CEM’s 84 .7 while VIS-score still ranks that task’s checkpoints at +0 .76 . A planner that finds much worse plans therefore still finds better plans with a better model. Cross-task comparability behaves differently. Pooled association and calibration remain strong for CEM ( +0 .81 , 9.4 points), MPPI ( +0 .81 , 17 .4), iCEM ( +0 .87 , 6.9) and multi-start gradient descent (+0 .86 , 10 .7), and weaken for predictive sampling ( +0 .51 , 26 .4) and single-start gradient descent (+0 .12 , 16 .2). The two failures are not a search-family effect—one is a sampler and one is a gradi-ent method—and by the previous paragraph they are not caused by the planners simply being weak. What the two share is that their weakness is task-selective : predictive sampling loses 41 .3 success points on PushT but only 4.3 and 3.3 on Reacher and Two-Room, while single-start gradient de-scent loses 29 .3 on Two-Room and 17 .0 on PushT but 3.7 on Reacher. A pooled correlation and a cross-task calibration map both require the tasks’ success levels to stay mutually comparable, and a planner that fails on one task and not another moves that task’s labels without moving its scores. MPPI is the counter-case that prevents us from stating this as a rule: its per-task deficits span 20 

points and its pooled correlation is nonetheless +0 .81 , so a task-selective deficit is a warning sign rather than a sufficient condition.
