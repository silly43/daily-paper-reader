Summary 37 

Brains rely on internal models of the world to interpret sensory input and to simulate the 38 

future. In the mammalian hippocampal-entorhinal network, environments are represented by 39 

cognitive maps that contain spatially selective neurons such as place, grid, head direction and 40 

object-vector cells 1-6 . Neurons with allocentric spatial tuning have recently been discovered also 41 

in non-mammalian organisms including larval zebrafish 7 but it remains unclear to what extent 42 

these neurons establish internal cognitive representations. We measured neuronal activity in 43 

telencephalic area Dc of head-fixed adult zebrafish exploring a novel, richly structured virtual 44 

reality. Neurons were sharply tuned to one or multiple locations and collectively represented 45 

environmental space. Activity fields exhibited neuron-specific associations to visual landmarks, 46 

indicating a prominent vectorial component in spatial representations. Population activity 47 

evolved and became increasingly informative as fish explored the environment. When landmarks 48 

were removed after familiarization, landmark-associated activity partially persisted and subsets 49 

of neurons reported prediction errors, implying that activity was in part driven by an internal 50 

representation. Strong functional coupling among neuronal ensembles and winner-take-all 51 

dynamics suggest that representations evolve by refinements of pre-structured networks. The 52 

teleost brain therefore generates internal models of structured environments that are optimized by 53 

experience and enable cognitive inference and prediction. 54

Introduction 56 

Cognition relies on internal models of the world that are established by evolution and by 57 

an individual’s experience. Concepts of such “cognitive maps” have been strongly influenced by 58 

representations of physical space in hippocampal-entorhinal networks of mammals 8-10 . These 59 

networks contain neurons such as place cells 1 , grid cells 2,3 , head direction cells 4 , object-vector 60 

cells 5,6 and other functional neuron types 11-14 that collectively represent an environment and the 61 

location of an animal within it. During navigation, network activity is updated on a moment-to-62 

moment basis by integrating multisensory inputs from internal (e.g., proprioceptive input evoked 63 

by body movements) and external sources (e.g., visual input evoked by movements relative to 64 

landmarks), tracking the animal’s trajectory over time. Depending on brain state, the same 65 

networks can also generate population activity that is not directly driven by sensory input and 66 

encodes information about past trajectories 15 , future trajectories 16 or objects that have been 67 

removed from an environment 11 . Hence, hippocampal-entorhinal networks represent relations 68 

between an animal and its environment also during memory recall, prediction, or planning, 69 

implying retrieval of information from internal models during cognitive brain functions. 70 

Moreover, hippocampal-entorhinal networks can establish cognitive maps of non-spatial 71 

information 17 and mediate episodic memory 18,19 . Studies using more complex environments and 72 

tasks indicate that neurons frequently exhibit mixed selectivity and conjunctive responses to 73 

different types of inputs 20-24 . Spatial cognitive maps in the hippocampal-entorhinal loop have 74 

thus become a leading model to study biological neuronal networks underlying memory and 75 

cognition. 76 

Computational models show that latent representations of physical space with place cell-77 

like units may, in principle, be generated by different mechanisms and network architectures 25-31 .78

In biological networks, cognitive maps of spatial environments have been described primarily in 79 

mammals. Recently, however, neurons with allocentric spatial tuning – referred to as place cells 80 

– were discovered also in the forebrain of food-caching birds 32 . Moreover, representations of 81 

boundaries, head direction, and locomotion variables were found in the goldfish 82 

telencephalon 33,34 , and an abundance of place cells was found in the telencephalon of larval 83 

zebrafish 7 . Zebrafish diverged from the mammalian lineage approximately 450 million years 84 

ago, yet place cells in larval zebrafish integrate spatial information from visual cues and self-85 

motion, and their place fields remap in different environments 7 , as observed in mammalian 86 

hippocampus. In addition, neurons encoding heading direction, which is critical for updating 87 

positional information during navigation, have been found in the anterior hindbrain of zebrafish 88 

larvae 35-37 . The larval zebrafish telencephalon therefore records an animal’s position as it 89 

navigates an environment using functional neuron types related to those found in mammals. 90 

However, further information is needed to examine whether spatial representations in mammals 91 

and teleosts share common algorithms and mechanisms. Importantly, it remains to be determined 92 

to what extent the teleost brain establishes internal, memory-based representations of 93 

environments that can be accessed in the absence of sensory input to support prediction and other 94 

cognitive computations. 95 

We studied spatial maps of visual environments in the telencephalon of adult zebrafish 96 

using a high-resolution closed-loop virtual reality (VR). We focused on a distinct subregion of 97 

pallial area Dc, which is thought to be phylogenetically related to mammalian cortex 38,39 . To 98 

determine how representations of environments are shaped by experience we examined neuronal 99 

population activity as animals repeatedly explored an initially novel, naturalistic corridor with 100 

visual landmarks. We discovered internal representations of environments that evolved with 101

Spatially selective neurons in cDc 111 

We analyzed representations of spatial environments in the telencephalon of adult 112 

zebrafish, which have ~100 times more neurons than larvae and exhibit a broader repertoire of 113 

cognitive behaviors 40 . Head-fixed adult zebrafish were swimming through a linear virtual 114 

corridor made of naturalistic textures (rocks, pebbles) with symmetrical, visually complex 115 

landmarks (2D images of aquatic creatures) on the walls (Fig. 1a). Four variations of this 116 

corridor were used that differed in the number and spatial arrangement of visual landmarks: 117 

7Reg (seven regularly spaced landmarks), 5Reg (five regularly spaced landmarks), 5Irr (five 118 

irregularly spaced landmarks), and 0Lm (no landmarks). Navigation was restricted to the forward 119 

direction, and fish were teleported back to the beginning of the corridor after each traversal (trial; 120 

Supplementary Video 1; 48 ± 31 s/trial [mean ± s.d.]; median: 43 s; inter-quartile range: 57 s – 121 

29 s). Fish had no prior experience with these or similar environments and no rewards or 122 

punishments were delivered. 123

Neuronal activity was measured by 2-photon calcium imaging through the intact skull 124 

using a transgenic line that expressed the calcium indicator GCaMP6f throughout the dorsal 125 

telencephalon (Methods). Measurements were targeted to a distinct subregion in the caudal part 126 

of telencephalic area Dc, referred to as cDc, that was identified reliably across individuals based 127 

on salient anatomical landmarks 41,42 (Fig. 1c,d, Methods). The field of view contained, on 128 

average, 320 ± 53 somata per fish (mean ± s.d., n = 14 fish) (Fig. 1d). Activity ( ∆F/F 0 ) was 129 

recorded during 15 – 30 min long sessions (Fig. 1g). In a subset of fish (n = 4), activity was 130 

recorded in two sessions separated by 40 – 147 min (see below). In two additional fish used for 131 

landmark deletion experiments (see below), activity was recorded in 4 or 5 sessions. The closed-132 

loop configuration of the VR was maintained throughout experiments (Fig. 1a,b,f). Each fish was 133 

exposed to one virtual environment (7Reg: 2fish; 5Reg: 4 fish; 5Irr: 6 fish, 0Lm: 2 fish). Activity 134 

measurements therefore captured early stages in the formation of representations of novel 135 

environments. 136 

Subsets of neurons showed pronounced activity at specific locations in the virtual 137 

corridor (Fig. 1h). To systematically identify spatially modulated neurons, we spatially binned 138 

activity in each trial to obtain spatial activity maps and identified peaks that were statistically 139 

significant across trials (Methods). The spatial information content of neurons with at least one 140 

significant peak was then quantified using a well-established measure of spatial specificity 141 

(Methods). Only neurons whose spatial specificity exceeded the expectation based on shuffle 142 

controls by at least three standard deviations were further analyzed for statistical significance of 143 

activity peaks on a trial-by-trial basis (Methods). We defined activity fields as the spatial extent 144 

exceeding half-height around each significant peak. Neurons with at least one activity field were 145 

defined as spatially selective (Fig. 1h). 146

Out of 3311 analyzed neurons of fish exposed to VR configurations with landmarks (n = 147 

10 fish), 461 (14%) were spatially selective (Fig. 2a, Extended Data Fig. 1a,b), with either one 148 

(246 neurons, 53%) or multiple (215 neurons, 47%) activity fields (Fig. 1h, 2e,f; Extended Data 149 

Figs. 1c, 2a). In the 0Lm environment, the fraction of neurons with fields was substantially lower 150 

(3%) and fields appeared broader (Fig. 2h; Extended Data Fig. 2b). To assess whether cDc 151 

neurons primarily represent spatial location or time, we analyzed activity also as a function of 152 

time relative to trial onset. Temporal activity maps were less well aligned across trials than 153 

spatial activity maps (Fig. 2f,g) and temporal specificity was significantly lower than spatial 154 

specificity of the same neurons ( P = 2.08 x 10 -73 , Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test) (Fig. 2a, inset). 155 

We therefore conclude that cDc neurons represent primarily spatial rather than temporal 156 

information. 157 

We next trained a neural network to decode spatial position from neural activity on a 158 

trial-by-trial basis in environments with landmarks (Methods). For each fish, we used the full set 159 

of spatially selective neurons, as well as subsampled sets of neurons (n = 16) to equalize counts 160 

across fish. In both cases, decoding performance was significantly higher than expectations 161 

derived from shuffled activity traces (Fig. 2b, Extended Data Fig. 1d), confirming that population 162 

activity enables positional decoding in individual fish. We further observed that fish with a 163 

higher number of spatially selective neurons had, on average, fewer fields per neuron (Pearson 164

between the number of spatially selective neurons and decoder performance (Pearson correlation 166

Representations of structured environments 172 

Our observations from VR environments with and without landmarks indicate that 173 

landmark-driven inputs contribute significantly, although not exclusively, to the spatial 174 

selectivity of cDc neurons, consistent with the contribution of both visual and self-motion 175 

information to spatial selectivity in the mammalian hippocampus and neocortex 43,44 . To further 176 

examine the influence of landmarks we first analyzed the autocorrelation function of spatial 177 

activity maps in two environments with different landmark configurations, 7Reg and 5Irr. If 178 

activity is driven by landmarks, the average autocorrelation should exhibit a modulation 179 

corresponding to the autocorrelation of the landmark pattern. Consistent with these expectations, 180 

autocorrelation functions of spatial activity maps had multiple peaks at locations predicted by the 181 

landmark autocorrelations (Fig. 3a,b). 182 

The autocorrelation indicates that activity fields of individual neurons are linked to 183 

landmarks, but it does not reveal the precise spatial relationship between fields and landmarks. 184 

To address this question, we divided each corridor into non-overlapping sectors that were 185 

delimited by the end of each landmark. Each activity field was then assigned a “landmark 186 

distance” defined as the distance between the activity peak and the end of the corresponding 187 

sector (Fig. 3c). The majority of neurons had at most one activity field per sector. In some cases, 188 

two fields were observed in the same sector, in particular in the 5Irr environment in sector 3, 189 

where the distance between landmarks was large. In this environment, the maximum landmark 190

To examine how activity fields covered space within sectors we analyzed the distribution 214 

of characteristic landmark distances across neurons and sectors. The distribution was broad 215 

without an obvious pattern (Fig. 3f), indicating that cDc neurons collectively cover a wide range 216 

of landmark distances, spanning the entire sector space. To examine spatial coverage of the full 217 

environment we sorted spatial activity maps by their maximum amplitude field and found that 218 

activity fields across the neural population covered the entire corridor (Fig. 3g). To further 219 

characterize spatial coverage, we generated a binarized representation of space by thresholding 220 

each neuron’s highest-amplitude field at half maximum (Fig. 3h and i). Hence, each spatially 221 

selective neuron contributed one segment with a defined location and width to the binarized 222 

representation of the corridor. We then randomly subsampled neurons and analyzed space 223 

coverage as a function of neuron number (Methods). The increase in space coverage with neuron 224 

number was not changed substantially after randomizing the positions of binary segments (Fig. 225 

3j,k). Hence, the distribution of activity fields along the corridor was almost indistinguishable 226 

from a random distribution, indicating that activity fields collectively covered the environment 227 

without obvious patterning by landmarks or other discontinuities. 228

Functional neuronal ensembles in cDc 337 

Spatial selectivity may emerge in a broad spectrum of networks with different 338 

architectures including generic, initially unstructured recurrent networks and networks with 339 

specific pre-configured connectivity 25-31,45 . In highly structured networks, subsets of neurons 340 

may be expected to form functional ensembles because they receive similar feed-forward and/or 341 

recurrent inputs. To explore this possibility we examined correlated variability of neuronal 342 

activity. Spatially selective activity of many cDc neurons varied substantially across trials, 343 

sometimes switching abruptly between high activity and silence in successive trials (Fig. 6f–n). 344 

To examine whether these variations were coordinated across the population we quantified 345 

pairwise trial-to-trial (noise) correlations between the signal magnitude of all fields in each of the 346

Discussion 397 

Our results revealed cognitive spatial maps of structured environments in an anatomically 398 

well-defined subregion of telencephalic area Dc in adult zebrafish. cDc neurons had one or 399 

multiple sharply delineated activity fields that may be referred to as place fields and collectively 400 

tracked an individual’s position. Spatially selective activity was strongly reduced but not 401 

abolished in the absence of landmarks, indicating that positional information is computed 402 

primarily from relations to landmarks with some contribution from path integration. Consistent 403 

with this conclusion, individual neurons represented specific distances to landmarks. In mice, 404 

similar distance coding in linear virtual environments is characteristic for neurons such as object-405 

vector cells in medial entorhinal cortex 5,13 . In two-dimensional environments, these and related 406 

neuron types represent distances and directions towards objects, giving rise to a vectorial code of 407 

space that is thought to be critical for navigation 6,12,14,47 . Our results thus provide evidence for 408 

vectorial representations of spatial environments in teleosts, although further experiments are 409 

required to examine spatial selectivity of cDc neurons in two-dimensional environments. 410 

Previous experiments in zebrafish revealed neurons with place fields in the telencephalon 411 

of freely swimming 7 but not head-fixed zebrafish larvae. Potentially important distinctions of our 412 

approach from previous studies using head-fixation include (1) the use of adult zebrafish, (2) the 413 

use of a high-resolution VR with naturalistic textures and objects, and (3) an efficient algorithm 414 

for closed-loop VR updates that supports naturalistic swimming 42 .415 

Under these conditions, spatially selective activity was reliably observed in Dc, which 416 

has been loosely linked to isocortex 38,39 . Functional properties of cDc neurons are reminiscent of 417 

cue-locking, object-vector and object-trace cells in the medial and lateral entorhinal cortex 5,11,13 ,418 

suggesting that cDc may be related to entorhinal cortex. However, cDc neurons also share 419

functional features with neurons in other brain areas including landmark vector and place cells in 420 

hippocampus 1,12,14,43 . Further studies are therefore needed to explore phylogenetic and functional 421 

relationships between cDc and mammalian brain areas. 422 

Few previous studies examined the emergence of spatial cognitive maps during the initial 423 

exploration of a novel environment. Consistent with observations in rodents 48-50 we found 424 

prominent spatially selective activity covering the entire environment already at early stages of 425 

exploration. Subsequently, representations were reorganized on a timescale of minutes to hours, 426 

resulting in increased selectivity of individual neurons and more informative population activity. 427 

While the spatial position of activity fields remained stable, their signal magnitude changed over 428 

time. Individual fields could appear de novo, as observed in hippocampal neurons after current 429 

injection 51 , or disappear abruptly between trials. These observations are consistent with models 430 

of pre-configured circuits that mediate initial spatial representations and a subsequent refinement 431 

of functional connectivity by experience, possibly by a self-organizing process that optimizes 432 

coding efficiency 45,52,53 .433 

We found small functional ensembles of co-tuned cDc neurons that were identified by a 434 

striking covariability of activity fluctuations. Instances of highly correlated activity within 435 

ensembles, but anti-correlated across ensembles, indicate that population activity exhibited 436 

winner-take-all dynamics consistent with cross-ensemble inhibition 46 , further supporting the 437 

notion of pre-configured circuit structure. In theory, such an organization is consistent with 438 

computational models that explicitly assume inhibition between small and distinct computational 439 

modules 30 . The small size of the zebrafish brain offers the opportunity to address structural 440 

predictions of such models by combining activity measurements with connectomics 54 .441

Manipulations of the virtual environment allowed us to directly examine whether the 442 

zebrafish brain establishes internal representations of an environment that enable computations 443 

underlying cognitive processes such as inference and prediction. Consistent with this hypothesis, 444 

we found traces of previous spatially selective activity after landmark deletion, implying that 445 

landmark-based spatial relationships were inferred from an internal model. Moreover, landmark 446 

deletion triggered prominent error signals, which requires the comparison of sensory input to an 447 

internally generated expectation 55-57 . Furthermore, conjunctive responses of individual neurons 448 

are consistent with the notion that internal representation and prediction error computations are 449 

closely inter-related, as one requires the other. Taken together, our results show that the zebrafish 450 

brain generates internal models of structured environments that are accessed for cognitive 451 

computations, consistent with a conservation of higher brain functions and the underlying 452 

mechanisms across vertebrates. 453

Methods 456 

Animal models. Experiments were performed in adult (6 – 18 months old) 457 

Tg(neuroD:GCaMP6f) zebrafish 58 (Danio rerio) of either sex in a nacre background. Animals 458 

were raised and kept under standard laboratory conditions (26 – 27 °C, 13 h/11 h light/dark 459 

cycle). All animal procedures were approved by the Veterinary Department of the Canton Basel-460 

Stadt, Switzerland. 461 

462 

Head-fixation. Head fixation was performed as described in Huang et al. 42 . Briefly, adult 463 

zebrafish were anesthetized by immersion in tricain methanesulfonate (MS-222; 0.03%) and held 464 

in a custom-build gyroscopic device. Anesthesia was maintained throughout the surgical 465 

procedure by perfusion with fish water containing MS-222 (0.01%) through a cannula inserted 466 

into the mouth. After removing the skin over the telencephalon and adjacent bones, light-weight, 467 

stainless-steel head bars made from syringe needles were attached to specific bones of the skull 468 

using tissue glue and dental cement. Fish were mounted by gluing the pins to a pair of vertical 469 

posts integrated into the floor of a transparent semi-hexagonal tank for submersion in the VR. 470 

After mounting, the anesthetic was washed out and fish recovered from anesthesia within ~1 471 

min. 472

Virtual reality . VR experiments were performed as described 42 with some modifications (see 474 

below). The tank with the mounted fish was placed under a custom-built multiphoton microscope 475 

with resonant scanners controlled by custom-written software based on Scanimage 58,59 . Infrared 476 

LEDs were placed outside the tank to acquire images of the tail with a video camera from below 477 

at a resolution of 90 x 70 pixels (medio-lateral x rostro-caudal) and a frame rate of 50 Hz. The 478 

VR scene was projected onto the three planar walls of the fish tank using three projectors. 479 

480 

The shape of the tail was tracked continuously (50 Hz) using custom LabVIEW code based on 481 

Huang et al. 42 . Forward movement was inferred from image frames based on the curvature and 482 

undulation of the caudal part of the tail as described 42 . Using custom software written in Python, 483 

the inferred forward movements were used to update the projection of the VR scene and to 484 

deploys user-defined protocols such as transient landmark deletions (see below). 485 

The Python-based game engine Panda3D was used to update the location of the virtual cameras 486 

in the VR. A set of three virtual cameras was implemented to capture three adjacent 45° x 60° 487 

(height by width) fields of view side by side (total of 45° x 180°, height by width). The geometry 488 

of the virtual world was constructed using the 3D modelling software Blender. 489 

Some modifications were made compared to the VR codebase described previously in Huang et 490 

al. 42 . The basic control of virtual cameras as a function of behaviour (tail images) was 491 

implemented as described 42 but the underlying Python codebase was redesigned for modular, 492 

combinatorial, and scalable control of all aspects of the VR geometry. This was achieved through 493 

the implementation of a Finite State Machine architecture which deploys VR protocols within an 494 

experimental data acquisition and trial structure framework ( https://github.com/fmi-495 

basel/DynCogRep-VR ). Additionally, the new codebase incorporates functionality for recording 496 

all relevant VR events that occur during an experiment, generating a detailed log for subsequent 497

Calcium imaging . Calcium imaging was performed using a modified 2-photon microscope 506 

(Sutter MOM scope) equipped with a x16 Nikon objective as described 42 . Images were acquired 507

Spatial activity fields . To define spatial activity fields of cDc neurons it is important to take into 587 

account the following observations: (1) individual neurons often have multiple fields, (2) trial-to-588 

trial variability of activity can be high, (3) activity within fields may change in intensity during 589 

an imaging session, (4) individual fields may appear de novo or disappear during a session. Since 590 

commonly used procedures may not robustly detect activity fields under these conditions, we 591 

used a multi-step procedure which includes some aspects of previously used methods 7,63 .592 

Spatial activity traces of each neuron were low-pass filtered as described above, generating a 593 

smoothed trace used for detecting all peaks and valleys. The magnitude of each peak was 594 

quantified by averaging values within a neighborhood of two space bins in each direction. Peak 595 

magnitudes were then standardized relative to the distribution of values in the entire spatial 596 

activity map, as described above. Magnitudes therefore represented the activity associated with 597 

each peak relative to an estimate of baseline activity in units of standard deviations. Peaks with a 598 

magnitude ≥5 were considered as candidates for fields and subjected to further analysis. Neurons 599 

without candidate fields were excluded from further analyses. 600 

We then quantified the spatial information of neurons with at least one candidate field. As 601 

described above, neurons whose spatial specificity did not exceed the mean of the corresponding 602 

null distribution by at least three standard deviations were discarded from any further analyses. 603 

In the next step, we quantified the amplitude and width of each candidate field. Amplitude was 604 

defined as the value of the smoothed spatial activity trace at the peak after subtracting the mean 605 

value of all valleys. Field width was measured as the width at half height around a peak. The 606 

spatial extent of a field was defined as the section of the corridor where the smoothed spatial 607 

activity trace around a peak exceeded half height, which could be asymmetric. When spatial 608 

extents of different candidate fields overlapped, only the candidate field with the maximum 609 

amplitude was kept. Fields were required to have positive amplitudes and half-heights within the 610 

location of the corridor. The latter was not always the case when fields were located close to the 611 

beginning or end of the corridor. Candidate fields that did not fulfill these criteria were excluded 612 

from further analysis. 613

Decoding of fish position from neural activity. Decoding was performed using a 4-layer 635 

feedforward artificial neural network (ANN) set up and trained using PyTorch. The ANN 636 

consisted of an input layer with number of nodes equal to the number of spatially tuned neurons, 637 

two hidden layers with 64 and 24 nodes, respectively, and a single output node. Activity traces 638 

and fish position from 60% of trials were used to train the neural network and the performance 639 

was evaluated on the unseen data. Training was performed over 200 epochs, within which 640 

performance was tracked using a subset of held-out trials (10%). This configuration of the neural 641 

network was determined empirically; minor changes to the architecture had only minor effects on 642 

performance and the overall trends were maintained. 643 

Decoding was performed either by including all spatially tuned neurons or by randomly 644 

subsampling neurons to equalize their numbers across fish to match the fish with the lowest 645 

count (16 neurons). Frames in which the fish were stationary (velocity <0.01 VR units/s) were 646 

excluded from decoding. Performance of the decoder was assessed by computing the average 647 

correlation coefficient between the decoded and true positions for a trial and by the average 648 

absolute error in decoded and true positions, expressed in units of VR space bins. The 649 

performance of the decoder was compared against decoders set up using shuffled activity traces, 650 

where the traces were independently shuffled in time for each neuron over a window spanning 651 

the length of the recording. 652

was split into the following categories: a) spatially tuned in S1 but not S2, b) tuned in both S1 656

generated after randomly reassigning neuron IDs across fields within corresponding sectors. In 686 

both cases, null distributions were based on 100 independent shuffles. 687 

Given the observed consistency of landmarks distances from fields of the same neuron we 688 

assigned a characteristic landmark distance to each neuron by computing the mean of the 689 

landmark distances of all of its fields. 690 

Spatial coverage. Spatial activity traces of neurons with at least one field were low-pass filtered 691 

as described above, normalized to their maxima, and ranked according to the location of their 692 

maxima. For analysis of coverage, we considered only the field with the highest amplitude of 693 

each neuron. Spatial activity traces were binarized by setting activity values within the spatial 694 

extent of the highest-amplitude field to 1 and all other activity values to 0. Space coverage was 695 

then calculated as the percentage of space bins that contained at least one non-zero entry across 696 

the population of neurons. Coverage as a function of neuron number was determined by drawing 697 

100 random subsets of neurons for each sample size. As a control, coverage was computed after 698 

assigning random spatial locations to each binarized field. This procedure was repeated 100 699 

times for each sample size. 700

activity during deletion (5 trials, DelLand window) to activity immediately preceding deletion (8 702 

trials, pre-DelLand window). The analysis was restricted to a small number of trials to minimize 703 

the potential influence of activity-dependent refinement processes independent of landmark 704 

deletion. Analysis procedures were adapted to conservatively detect activity fields given low trial 705 

numbers. 706

For each segment, we searched for peaks within the segments’ spatial extent on a trial-by-trial 716

overlapping activity segment was detected in the DelLand window. If this requirement was not 725 

fulfilled, activity segments were classified as “sensitive” to landmark deletion. Visual inspection 726 

of data indicates that this procedure is conservative, i.e., false-positive detections of persistent 727 

activity segments appeared to be rare. 728 

To assess whether the fraction of persistent activity segments was statistically different from 729 

chance we generated a null distribution based on 100 shuffled controls. Each control was 730 

generated by circular permutation of the original activity traces corresponding to each DelLand 731 

window trial. Trial-wise circular permutation of DelLand windows was performed for deletion 732 

events where the pre-DelLand window contained at least one activity segment. 733 

For some deletion events, we observed that activity during landmark deletion was not spatially 734 

localized, as typically observed in the presence of landmarks, but broad and intense (high-735 

intensity activity). To detect such responses, we identified deletion events with at least one 736 

activity segment before or during landmark deletion and quantified the ratio of the mean activity 737 

trace values during and before deletion. Activity was classified as “high-intensity” in the absence 738 

of landmarks relative to the presence of landmarks if the ratio was ≥2 . Such responses were 739 

considered to be prediction error signals and were analyzed separately. 740 

Identification of neuronal ensembles. To locate examples of functionally related sets of 741 

neurons, we first identified all inter-neuron field pairs complying with two criteria: 1) their trial-742 

wise noise correlation exceeded an absolute threshold (r ≥ 0.6 ), and 2) fields within each pair 743 

belonged to the same VR corridor sector. We refer to these field pairs as “high-pass field pairs”. 744 

We then searched for neuron sets of size 3 where each neuron was related to the other two 745 

neurons via high-pass field pairs. By identifying and merging trios with at least one shared 746 

neuron, we generated a list of unique non-overlapping sets of neurons (“super trios”). Finally, 747 

super trios were expanded by adding neurons directly related to any of their members via high-748 

pass field pairs. Such extended super trios correspond to identified functional ensembles. 749
