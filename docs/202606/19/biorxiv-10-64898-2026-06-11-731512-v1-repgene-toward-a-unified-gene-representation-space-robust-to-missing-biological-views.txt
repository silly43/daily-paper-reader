## Abstract 

Genes can be described through multiple heterogeneous biological views, includ-ing genomic sequence, transcript sequence, protein sequence, textual knowledge, and single-cell expression context, yet existing gene embeddings remain largely modality-specific and difficult to compare or reuse when many views are unavail-able. We study a narrower but practically important question: whether pretrained embeddings from these distinct sources can be organized into a shared gene repre-sentation interface that remains usable under severe missing-modality conditions. To investigate this question, we introduce RepGene , a lightweight single-branch framework that combines modality adapters, a shared encoder, presence-aware fusion, and self-supervised cross-view objectives to map five biological views into one latent space. Our goal is not to claim a new multimodal learning principle or to establish superiority over all simpler fusion strategies, but to provide an initial technical instantiation for testing whether such a shared interface is feasible in a fixed-feature setting. Under a two-stage protocol in which RepGene is trained self-supervised on frozen upstream embeddings and evaluated by downstream linear probing, we find preliminary evidence that the learned representation is broadly competitive in the full-modality setting and remains informative when only partial modality subsets are observed at inference time. The strongest signal in our study is robustness under missing views: average performance changes are often limited when one modality is removed, and even single-view inference remains non-trivial in the evaluated benchmark regime. These results do not resolve unified biologi-cal representation learning, and they should be interpreted in light of incomplete simple-fusion baselines, limited architectural ablation, benchmark dependence, and possible upstream feature exposure. We therefore position RepGene as a feasibility study and a starting point for stronger comparisons, broader benchmarks, and leakage-aware validation. 

## 1 Introduction 

Genes can be characterized through multiple biological views, including genomic sequence, transcript sequence, protein sequence, textual knowledge, and single-cell expression context. Recent biological foundation models make these views increasingly accessible, but the resulting embeddings typically remain separated into modality-specific spaces that are difficult to compare, compose, or reuse when only a subset of views is available.

This fragmentation creates a practical bottleneck for gene representation learning. In realistic settings, not every gene is observed through every modality, and downstream performance can depend as much on modality choice as on the learning architecture itself. This motivates a broader question than standard multimodal fusion: can heterogeneous pretrained gene embeddings be organized into a shared representation interface that remains usable under partial modality availability? In this work, we study that question through RepGene , a unified single-branch framework for missing-modality-robust gene representation learning. Rather than building a large modality-specific multi-tower system, RepGene uses lightweight adapters to normalize heterogeneous input dimensions, a shared encoder to process all views through one common pathway, presence-aware fusion to aggregate only observed modalities, and self-supervised objectives to encourage cross-view consistency. We emphasize from the outset that our goal is not to claim that unified biological representation learning is solved, nor that the present design is already superior to simpler fusion alternatives. Instead, we aim to provide an initial technical instantiation and empirical feasibility study of this research direction. A central design hypothesis in RepGene is that routing different modalities through one shared encoding pathway may encourage a more comparable latent geometry than keeping them in separate modality-specific branches. In the current paper, however, this should be read as a motivated architectural choice rather than a definitively isolated causal mechanism: our experiments are intended to test whether the overall shared-space formulation is workable in a fixed-feature setting, not to fully settle which architectural component is strictly necessary. Our evaluation follows a strict two-stage protocol. In stage one, RepGene is trained self-supervised using frozen foundation-model features and without access to downstream benchmark labels. In stage two, the learned representations are frozen and assessed using benchmark-defined linear probes under strict train–test splits. This setup is designed to evaluate representation informativeness rather than end-to-end supervised adaptation. Within this scope, we ask three focused questions: whether a shared-space model can produce competitive fused representations, whether the learned representation remains usable when modalities are missing at inference time, and whether multimodal shared-space training preserves meaningful single-view usability after joint learning. Among these, the missing-modality question is the most central, because practical gene-centered applications often face incomplete modality coverage rather than clean, fully observed multimodal records. We make the following contributions: • We formulate unified gene representation under severe missing-modality conditions as a concrete feasibility question over five heterogeneous biological views. • We present RepGene as a lightweight single-branch instantiation of this idea, combining modality adapters, a shared encoder, presence-aware fusion, and self-supervised alignment objectives in a fixed-feature regime. • We provide preliminary frozen-linear-probe evidence that the learned shared space remains informative across multiple task families and under substantial modality removal at inference time. • We explicitly delineate the current evidence boundary of this study, including incomplete simple-fusion baselines, limited architectural ablation, benchmark dependence, possible upstream feature exposure, and restricted organismal scope. Taken together, these contributions position RepGene as an initial step toward a unified gene represen-tation interface rather than a definitive solution. We therefore frame the paper as a feasibility-oriented study whose main value lies in clarifying what appears promising, what remains unresolved, and what stronger validation is still required. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Unimodal biological foundation models 

Large pretrained models have recently become available across multiple biological views spanning the central dogma and related knowledge channels [Crick, 1970]. In genomics, models such as Evo2, DNABERT-2, HyenaDNA, and the Nucleotide Transformer learn sequence representations 2

from DNA and genomic corpora [Brixi et al., 2026, Zhou et al., 2024, Dalla-Torre et al., 2023]. In transcriptomics and proteomics, LucaOne and protein language models such as ESM and ProtTrans provide rich embeddings from nucleotide or amino-acid sequences [He et al., 2025, Lin et al., 2023, Hayes et al., 2025, Elnaggar et al., 2021]. In parallel, text-derived gene representations and single-cell foundation models provide additional semantic and cellular-context views for the same biological entities [Chen and Zou, 2023, Alibaba Cloud, 2025, Cui et al., 2024, Theodoris et al., 2023, Yang et al., 2022, Hao et al., 2024]. These models greatly expand the design space of gene embeddings, but they also create a fragmented ecosystem. Each upstream model defines its own embedding geometry, training distribution, and biological bias, so users often choose one modality-specific embedding family per downstream task rather than operating over a common cross-view interface. 

2.2 Gene embedding benchmarks and modality heterogeneity 

Recent benchmark efforts show that gene-embedding quality can depend strongly on which biological view is used, and that inter-modality heterogeneity remains substantial even when intra-modality representations are relatively consistent. The Gene Benchmark work and subsequent benchmarking studies make this especially clear: modality choice is often a dominant determinant of downstream behavior, which motivates studying cross-view alignment rather than continuing to treat each view as an isolated silo [Kan-Tor et al., 2025, Zhong et al., 2023, 2025, Schultheiss et al., 2022, Du et al., 2019]. This benchmark landscape matters for our paper in two ways: it provides a practical evaluation protocol for frozen linear probes across diverse gene-understanding tasks, and it sharpens the central question of this work. Our goal is not to ask which single modality is universally best, but whether heterogeneous modalities can be mapped into a shared representation layer that remains useful even when views are missing. 

2.3 Multimodal integration in biology 

A growing body of work studies multimodal modeling in biology, including surveys of multimodal foundation models and task-specific fusion systems [Wang et al., 2025, Zheng et al., 2025a]. However, many multimodal designs remain multi-branch or modality-specialized, which can make them harder to scale under missing-modality settings and can blur whether they are learning a truly shared semantic interface or only a downstream fusion rule. Recent multimodal biological systems, including PRISME, sciLaMA, and BioLangFusion, further underscore the momentum of this direction [Zheng et al., 2025b, Hu et al., 2025, Mollaysa et al., 2025]. These efforts differ in modality coverage, task focus, evaluation setup, or public reproducibility status, so direct comparison is non-trivial. Our work is therefore framed as a feasibility study centered on one question: whether a minimal single-branch pathway can align heterogeneous gene embeddings into a common space. 

2.4 Missing-modality robust representation learning 

Outside biology, single-branch multimodal learning, shared embedding spaces, and masked recon-struction have shown that a unified pathway can sometimes provide robustness to partial observations [Girdhar et al., 2023, He et al., 2022, Chen et al., 2020, Oord et al., 2018, Mollaysa et al., 2024]. These ideas are especially relevant to our setting because gene-centered applications often face uneven modality availability rather than cleanly complete multimodal records. RepGene adopts this line of thinking, but adapts it to gene representation learning with heterogeneous upstream biological foundation models. The novelty we emphasize is not the use of adapters alone, but the attempt to induce one shared gene representation interface from five distinct biological views under missing-modality training.

We present RepGene, a single-branch framework for unified multimodal gene representation learning. The framework is designed to test a broader feasibility hypothesis: whether heterogeneous gene embeddings extracted from different biological foundation models can be aligned into one shared latent space that remains usable under severe missing-modality conditions. 3.4 Dynamic modality and feature masking 

To emulate realistic incomplete-view conditions, RepGene applies both modality-level and feature-level masking during training. For each mini-batch, we sample a masking pattern over originally available modalities that may hide zero, one, two, three, or four views for each gene, and within retained modalities we additionally mask a fraction of feature dimensions. If modality m is synthetically masked for gene g, we replace its encoder input by a zero vector and set pmg = 0 , training the model to form consistent gene representations from partial-view subsets rather than always-complete multimodal inputs [Mollaysa et al., 2024]. However, if that modality is originally available, omg = 1 is unchanged, so the original feature remains a reconstruction target through Lrec . Truly absent modalities satisfy omg = 0 and therefore contribute neither to fusion nor to reconstruction. 

3.5 Method boundary statement 

RepGene is intentionally a first-generation framework. It studies whether a unified representation interface can emerge from a minimal shared-pathway design with self-supervised objectives, but it does not by itself establish optimality over stronger simple-fusion baselines, alternative backbone combinations, or leakage-aware benchmark variants. We therefore present the method as a concrete feasibility probe for shared-space alignment rather than as a definitive formulation of multimodal biological representation learning. 

## 4 Feasibility Study and Preliminary Evaluation 

This section evaluates RepGene as a preliminary empirical study rather than a final performance contest. The goal is to assess whether a shared-space formulation can produce useful gene repre-sentations across diverse tasks and remain informative when substantial modality missingness is introduced at inference time. 

4.1 Experimental setup Upstream features. We use five pretrained embedding sources spanning genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, text, and single-cell transcriptomics, with input views given by DNA nucleotide se-quences, mRNA nucleotide sequences, amino-acid sequences, natural-language gene descriptions, and scRNA-seq expression profiles. These features are treated as fixed upstream representations, and RepGene operates on top of them without fine-tuning the underlying foundation models. 6

Benchmark and tasks. We evaluate on a gene-understanding benchmark that includes multiple task families such as genomic properties, regulatory functions, protein-related properties, functional annotations, and disease-associated prediction settings. The benchmark is used as a common frozen-representation test bed rather than as supervision for representation learning. 

Two-stage evaluation protocol. Our evaluation follows a strict two-stage paradigm. First, Rep-Gene is trained self-supervised using only pretrained modality features and without access to down-stream benchmark labels. Second, the learned gene representations are frozen and evaluated with benchmark-specified linear probes under strict train–test splits. This setup is intended to measure the informativeness of the learned shared representation space rather than supervised adaptation to each downstream task. 

Benchmark provenance and protocol scope. Benchmark provenance details and interpretive scope are summarized in Appendix H.1. We use the Gene Benchmark as a standardized linear-probing interface for feasibility evaluation rather than as the sole source of biological ground truth. 

4.2 Feasibility questions 

We organize the evaluation around three questions. 

Q1: Can a shared-space model produce competitive fused gene representations? This question asks whether a unified latent interface can remain informative across diverse tasks when all available modalities are fused through one shared pathway. 

Q2: Does the representation remain usable under partial modality subsets? This question targets the core missing-modality setting. We test whether the learned space remains informative when only a subset of the five views is available at inference time. 

Q3: Does shared-space training help single-view usability after multimodal learning? This question examines whether a representation learned jointly across views retains useful information even when queried from only one observed modality. 

4.3 Main observations 

Across the benchmark tasks, the results are consistent with the view that a shared-space formulation can be workable in the present evaluation regime. In the full-modality setting, RepGene yields fused representations that are broadly competitive with several unimodal feature sources while providing a single common representation interface rather than requiring task-by-task modality selection. Figure 2 shows that this behavior is not concentrated in only one task family. Instead, the fused representation remains reasonably competitive across genomic properties, regulatory functions, localization, biological processes, and protein-related tasks, which is consistent with the intended role of RepGene as a shared interface rather than a modality-specialized expert. 4.4 Interpretation and scope 

Taken together, the full-modality and partial-modality results provide preliminary evidence that a shared-space formulation can be viable within the present fixed-feature, frozen-linear-probe setting. The strongest support is for feasibility under missing modalities, not for the stronger claim that RepGene has already been shown to outperform simpler fusion alternatives or that its shared encoder has been conclusively validated as the decisive mechanism. This interpretation remains bounded by the current evidence. Evaluation is limited to one benchmark ecosystem, baseline coverage is still incomplete for simple fusion and broader subset comparisons, and the upstream encoders may encode benchmark-relevant information from pretraining. We therefore view the current study as an encouraging first step that motivates stronger baselines, broader ablations, and leakage-aware follow-up evaluation. 

## 5 Limitations 

This paper is intended as a feasibility study rather than a definitive validation of unified gene representation learning. The current results suggest that heterogeneous pretrained gene embeddings can be organized into a shared latent interface that remains informative under missing-modality conditions, but they do not establish that the present formulation is optimal. 8

Several limitations shape this interpretation. First, baseline coverage remains incomplete, especially for simple mean/concat fusion, broader 2–3 modality subsets, and fully matched raw single-modality comparisons, so the current evidence does not yet isolate gains from shared-space learning itself. Second, although RepGene is motivated by a shared-encoder design, the present ablations do not fully establish which architectural components are necessary. Third, evaluation is limited to one benchmark ecosystem, one fixed set of upstream feature providers, and a frozen-linear-probe setting, so broader generalization remains unresolved. Fourth, because the upstream models are pretrained on large biological corpora, sequences, and text collections, the results should be interpreted as evidence in a cascaded foundation-model setting rather than as leakage-free biological generalization. Taken together, these limitations mean that RepGene should be read as an encouraging but still incomplete first step toward unified gene representation. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We studied whether pretrained embeddings from five biological views can be organized into a shared gene representation interface that remains usable under severe missing-modality conditions. In this setting, RepGene provides a lightweight single-branch instantiation of that idea through modality adaptation, shared encoding, presence-aware fusion, and self-supervised cross-view training. Across frozen-linear-probe evaluations, the results provide preliminary evidence that such a shared-space formulation can remain broadly informative in the full-modality setting and, more importantly, under substantial modality removal at inference time. We interpret this as support for the feasibility of unified gene representation in a fixed-feature regime, not as a definitive demonstration that the present formulation is optimal or that its individual architectural components are fully validated. More broadly, our findings suggest that a common interface across heterogeneous biological foundation-model embeddings may be achievable, but stronger simple baselines, broader archi-tectural ablations, alternative upstream encoders, and leakage-aware benchmarks remain necessary. We therefore view RepGene as an encouraging first step rather than a settled endpoint. 

## 7 Broader Impact 

This work explores whether heterogeneous biological foundation-model embeddings can be aligned into a unified representation interface for genes. If such interfaces become reliable, they may help reduce fragmentation across biological data modalities, simplify downstream model selection, and support more flexible computational pipelines for hypothesis generation in functional genomics and biotechnology. At the same time, this direction carries several risks. First, a unified representation may encourage over-trust in compact embeddings whose apparent generality exceeds their actual evidence base, especially when upstream models inherit biases from pretraining corpora or when downstream benchmarks only partially reflect real biological complexity. Second, biological representation systems may be misused in high-stakes settings such as disease prioritization or therapeutic reasoning without sufficient domain validation. For these reasons, we do not present RepGene as a deployable biomedical decision system. We instead view it as a research prototype whose outputs should be interpreted with caution, evaluated under leakage-aware protocols, and used only with appropriate biological oversight. More generally, any future release of shared biological embedding systems should be accompanied by clear documentation of training assets, benchmark scope, and known failure modes.

A.2 Shared encoder 

After adaptation, each modality is processed by the same transformer encoder E. The shared encoder uses two transformer layers, four attention heads, a feed-forward hidden size of 512, dropout of 0.3, and GELU activations. Because all modalities pass through the same encoder parameters, the model is encouraged to process them through one common latent pathway.

A.4 Decoders 

Each modality uses a decoder Dm that mirrors the corresponding adapter architecture. These decoders are used only during self-supervised training to reconstruct masked or missing feature views from the fused representation. 

A.5 Why the shared encoder matters 

A potential misreading is that RepGene merely projects all modalities to the same dimensionality and then combines them. That is not the intended mechanism: the adapters only map heterogeneous backbone outputs into a common hidden size, whereas the shared encoder exposes every modality to the same parameterized transformation and forces them through a common semantic pathway. This pathway interacts directly with the training objectives. Inter-modal contrastive learning pulls views of the same gene closer in latent space, masked-view reconstruction requires the fused repre-sentation to preserve information useful for recovering originally available but synthetically hidden inputs, and presence-aware fusion trains the model to form stable gene-level representations from variable modality subsets. The shared encoder is therefore not a cosmetic dimensionality-matching device, but the component through which these pressures act jointly to shape a common latent geometry.

## C Evaluation Protocol Notes 

C.1 Two-stage paradigm 

The evaluation protocol is designed to isolate representation quality from task-specific supervised training. In stage one, RepGene is trained using self-supervised objectives on pretrained modality features only. In stage two, the learned gene representations are frozen and passed to linear probes defined on benchmark train–test splits. This protocol is important because it prevents downstream labels from directly shaping the represen-tation model. As a result, the reported scores should be interpreted as evidence about representation informativeness rather than end-to-end task optimization. 

C.2 Interpretive scope 

The benchmark used in this paper is a practical test bed for feasibility evaluation, but it should not be treated as exhaustive. The current results support cautious conclusions about shared-space viability in one benchmarked setting, not universal claims across all biological data regimes.

## E Leakage-aware Notes 

The evaluation in this paper should be interpreted with awareness that the upstream foundation models may have been pretrained on broad corpora that overlap semantically, and in some cases structurally, with concepts used in downstream benchmarks. We therefore do not claim that the current benchmark fully isolates biological generalization from possible upstream feature exposure. A stronger future evaluation protocol would pair shared-space learning with leakage-aware dataset curation, explicit temporal or source-level separation where possible, and direct comparison against simpler fusion baselines under the same train–test splits. We include this note to mark the intended evidence boundary of the current study rather than to imply that such controls have already been fully implemented.
